What happens when you press Make my retreat
  1. Reading your document (a few seconds). The text and any pictures are taken out of it.
  2. Planning (1 to 3 minutes). A model reads the whole thing and decides how many days there are, which passage belongs to each day (copied word for word), a grace to ask for, a focus, and which picture goes with which day. If your document already has days, it follows them.
  3. Making each day (2 to 5 minutes a day, in order). A reflection for the heart is written; then the passage is researched on the web and a deep dive is written that knows what the reflection said; then the spoken guidance is tailored to both. Every part is recorded as soon as its words are ready.
  4. Ready to pray. A week usually takes 15 to 30 minutes in all. You can close the page or lock your phone; it keeps going, and the retreat will be in your library.

Each day you'll pray with the reading four times, lectio divina style, with the reflection and deep dive between the readings, a time of silence, and your own prayer at the end. Nothing is published; your retreats are private to your account.

            About Ignatius at Home

            Ignatius at Home turns prayer material you already have (a retreat handout, scripture passages, a reading, images) into a guided audio retreat you can pray at home, one day at a time.

            The Spiritual Exercises

            Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), the founder of the Jesuits, wrote the Spiritual Exercises as a guide for leading someone through a path of prayer; Pope Paul III approved them in 1548. They are arranged in four "weeks," stages rather than calendar weeks: God's love and mercy, the life of Christ, his passion, and his resurrection. Each time of prayer begins by asking for a particular grace, often enters a scene from the Gospels with the imagination, and ends in a colloquy, speaking to God "as one friend speaks to another." Along the way you learn to notice consolation and desolation, the movements of the heart toward and away from God, which is the root of Ignatian discernment.

            What a retreat is

            A retreat is time set apart for prayer. The full Exercises can be made in about thirty days of silence at a retreat house, but Ignatius also allowed them to be made in daily life: at home over several months, with a set time of prayer each day and regular conversations with a spiritual director. Many parishes and Jesuit ministries run retreats like this through the year, week by week, with a handout of scripture and readings for each week. Ignatius at Home is built for that rhythm, and a retreat can continue from week to week as a series.

            Lectio divina

            Lectio divina, "divine reading," is the old monastic way of praying slowly with scripture. In the twelfth century the Carthusian monk Guigo II described its steps as a ladder: lectio, reading the text; meditatio, pondering what it says to me; oratio, answering God in prayer; and contemplatio, resting in God. Pope Benedict XVI's Verbum Domini (2010) adds actio, carrying the word into life.

            How a day is prayed here

            1. Ask for the grace of the day, then a short silence.
            2. First reading: simply listen (lectio).
            3. For the heart: a reflection that stays with the passage and invites you to notice what stirs.
            4. Second reading: listen for what it says to you (meditatio).
            5. Deep dive: the passage's setting, words, and how the church has read it.
            6. Third reading: listen for what God may offer or ask.
            7. Silence between two bells, resting with a word (contemplatio).
            8. Last reading, then speak to God in your own words (oratio, the colloquy), and close.

            The voices

            Every part of a day is read aloud by a synthetic voice, and you can choose a different voice for the guide, the reading, the reflection and the deep dive. Each sample below reads the same lines: John 20:16 from the World English Bible, then a line of spoken guidance.

            Jesus said to her, "Mary." She turned and said to him, "Rabboni!" which is to say, "Teacher!" Take a moment now. Let the name spoken to you settle in your heart, and notice what stirs.

            Free: Microsoft neural voices

            Read through Microsoft's Edge "Read aloud" service (the open-source edge-tts package). They cost nothing and need no key, are clear and even, and handle scripture well. Their weaknesses: the pacing is steadier and less expressive than a human reader, long scripts are recorded in 400-character pieces so the tone can shift a little between sentences, and because the service is unofficial it occasionally drops a request (the app retries, with backoff).

            Premium: ElevenLabs

            ElevenLabs voices breathe, slow down and lower their voice the way a person does when reading something that matters, and they hold one tone across a whole reflection (the app sends 2,500-character pieces with the text before each piece, so the joins are seamless). For prayer that difference is real: a reflection meant to be listened to in silence sounds less like an announcement and more like someone sitting with you. They cost money: about 10,000–15,000 characters for a day, charged against the account's monthly credits, so they are for premium accounts; everyone can hear them in the premium example retreat.

            Conversation voices

            Talk it over uses live speech-to-speech models rather than recorded audio. OpenAI GPT-Live connects your browser straight to OpenAI over WebRTC, with very low delay and natural turn-taking; it costs about 5¢ a minute. xAI Grok voice streams audio over a WebSocket with a short-lived key from the server, has a choice of distinct voices (Eve, Ara, Rex, Sal, Leo), and costs about 8¢ a minute. The server never sends your key-holding credentials to the browser, only a single-use session.

            Hear the conversation voices

            Each says the same line: "Hello. I'm glad you're here. What stayed with you from today's prayer?" These were recorded with each company's text-to-speech. In a live conversation the same voice sounds a little more relaxed and conversational. OpenAI's other live voices (Vesper, Willow, Quartz, Meridian, Stone, Gleam, Beacon, Delta, Cinder and Ripple) are only available live, so they can't be sampled here; start a conversation to hear them.

            How the companion talks: live voices and taking turns

            Talk it over can work in two quite different ways. The live voices (OpenAI GPT-Live and xAI Grok) are one model that hears and speaks. Taking turns chains three separate steps: your device turns speech into text, a text model (the "brain") writes a reply, and a synthetic voice reads it aloud. Each is better at something.

            Why the live voices feel so human

            A live voice model works on sound itself. Your voice is streamed to it continuously (over WebRTC for OpenAI, a WebSocket for Grok), cut into small pieces of audio the model reads much as a text model reads words, and it answers with pieces of audio, streamed straight back. Nothing in between turns your speech into words and back. So it hears more than your words (hesitation, a catch in the voice, a laugh), and it can answer with more than words: the small "mm" and "mhm" of someone listening, a breath before a hard sentence, a softer tone when you sound tired. None of that was written down; it comes from the model having learned from real speech.

            It can be interrupted because it's always listening. The service watches the incoming sound for speech (voice activity detection). When you start talking while it's speaking, it stops, throws away the rest of what it was going to say, and listens; when you pause for a moment, it takes that as its turn. That is what makes it the most conversational way to talk with a model.

            What the live voices give up

            • Depth of thought. A speech-to-speech model has to start answering almost at once, so it doesn't stop to think the way a reasoning text model can, and it is generally not as strong a writer or reasoner as the best text models. Long instructions, your notes and the whole retreat are harder for it to keep in view.
            • Tools. The text models behind the rest of the app can be given tools, such as the web search the deep dive uses to check facts. The live voices here have none: they can only talk. (Their services can hand hard questions to a separate text model with tools, but that adds delay, and this app doesn't use it.)
            • Exact words. The transcript is the service's own guess at what was said, and following written instructions to the letter is harder for it.
            • Cost while quiet. A live conversation is billed for every second it's open, silence included: GPT-Live costs about five cents a minute whether or not anyone speaks.

            What taking turns does better, and what it gives up

            Taking turns is how most AI assistants you type to become voice assistants: speech to text, the model, then text to speech. Because the middle step is an ordinary text model, you can choose the strongest writer (Claude Fable or Opus) or a free one; it reads the companion's full instructions, your notes and the retreat day by day, and can think before it answers; and it costs only for what it reads and writes, so silence is free. The price is the conversation's texture. It waits until you've finished; you can't cut in mid-sentence; your tone of voice is lost when your speech becomes text; and the reply is read by a synthetic voice, which pronounces words well but won't say "mm" unless the words say so.

            A practical rule: for a flowing conversation, choose a live voice; for silence-heavy prayer, or for the most thoughtful replies, take turns. Choose under Change voice on Talk it over, or on the Agents page.

            The research services

            With the free open models, the deep dive is written from web research the server does first: the model proposes three searches, the services run them, and the model writes from what comes back, citing only pages that were actually returned. Claude models get the same results as a head start and then search the web themselves as much as they need. By default all services, combined are asked at once and their results are interleaved, one from each in turn, so no single index decides what the model reads. If a service runs out of monthly credits it's paused until next month and the others carry on; every query and result is logged. On a computer, the research behind each day is under "Research notes" on the retreat page.

            Brave's own independent web index (not a reseller of Google or Bing). Fast, broad, and good for mainstream reference pages. The snippets are short, so the model sees less of each page.

            Exa
            A search engine built for AI that searches by meaning rather than keywords, and returns highlighted passages. Strong at finding essays, commentaries and scholarly writing on an idea; weaker for exact phrases and very recent news.
            About 1,400 searches a month free ($10 of credit, reset on the 1st; no card). Get a key · Quickstart · Search reference · Pricing · key: EXA_API_KEY
            Tavily
            A search API made for AI agents that returns cleaned, relevance-ranked extracts of each page. Good all-round results with useful content; its free tier is a modest monthly number of searches.
