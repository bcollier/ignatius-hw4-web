          About Ignatius at Home

          Ignatius at Home turns prayer material you already have (a retreat handout, scripture passages, a reading, images) into a guided audio retreat you can pray at home, one day at a time.

          The Spiritual Exercises

          Ignatius of Loyola (1491–1556), the founder of the Jesuits, wrote the Spiritual Exercises as a guide for leading someone through a path of prayer; Pope Paul III approved them in 1548. They are arranged in four "weeks," stages rather than calendar weeks: God's love and mercy, the life of Christ, his passion, and his resurrection. Each time of prayer begins by asking for a particular grace, often enters a scene from the Gospels with the imagination, and ends in a colloquy, speaking to God "as one friend speaks to another." Along the way you learn to notice consolation and desolation, the movements of the heart toward and away from God, which is the root of Ignatian discernment.

          What a retreat is

          A retreat is time set apart for prayer. The full Exercises can be made in about thirty days of silence at a retreat house, but Ignatius also allowed them to be made in daily life: at home over several months, with a set time of prayer each day and regular conversations with a spiritual director. Many parishes and Jesuit ministries run retreats like this through the year, week by week, with a handout of scripture and readings for each week. Ignatius at Home is built for that rhythm, and a retreat can continue from week to week as a series.

          Lectio divina

          Lectio divina, "divine reading," is the old monastic way of praying slowly with scripture. In the twelfth century the Carthusian monk Guigo II described its steps as a ladder: lectio, reading the text; meditatio, pondering what it says to me; oratio, answering God in prayer; and contemplatio, resting in God. Pope Benedict XVI's Verbum Domini (2010) adds actio, carrying the word into life.

          How a day is prayed here

          1. Ask for the grace of the day, then a short silence.
          2. First reading: simply listen (lectio).
          3. For the heart: a reflection that stays with the passage and invites you to notice what stirs.
          4. Second reading: listen for what it says to you (meditatio).
          5. Deep dive: the passage's setting, words, and how the church has read it.
          6. Third reading: listen for what God may offer or ask.
          7. Silence between two bells, resting with a word (contemplatio).
          8. Last reading, then speak to God in your own words (oratio, the colloquy), and close.

          The spoken guidance, reflection and deep dive are written by AI models from your material, and the voices are synthetic. Treat them as a companion to your own prayer and, if you have one, your spiritual director, not a replacement.
